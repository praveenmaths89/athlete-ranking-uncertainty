# Introduction

Soccer academies and national talent programs select a few young players from a much larger pool and then invest years of coaching in them. In India, schemes such as Khelo India and the All India Football Federation's Blue Cubs league follow this pattern, and physical testing is one of the few objective inputs available at scale. The usual workflow is simple: test every player on a battery, convert the results into z-scores or percentiles, combine them into a composite score, and select the top *k* from the resulting leaderboard. A player ranked 36th is selected and a player ranked 38th is not.

That workflow treats small differences in rank as real, and its choices are rarely examined: which metrics to keep, how to weight them, whether a 12-year-old should be compared with a 25-year-old, and how to combine several visits. Talent research has long shown that relative age and biological maturation inflate the scores of older and earlier-maturing players [@cobley2009; @helsen2005; @malina2015; @hill2020], that talent identification has limited predictive validity [@vaeyens2008; @johnston2018], and that soccer talent studies often ignore measurement error [@bergkamp2019]. Outside sport, league tables of hospitals and schools change substantially once sampling error is considered [@goldstein1996], and statistical methods now exist to put confidence sets around ranks [@hall2009; @mogstad2024]. Yet physical-test leaderboards in youth soccer are still reported as bare rank orders. How uncertain those ranks are at the selection cut-off, and how many selections they get wrong, remains unmeasured.

This paper quantifies that uncertainty for force-plate jump testing and turns it into a selection rule. Our contributions are:

1. A benchmark of 15 combinations of ranking method and weighting scheme for building a composite from repeated tests, evaluated against a simulated ground truth and against players' unseen later visits.
2. An estimate of how much age adjustment, using growth-reference (LMS) scores, changes who is selected.
3. Rank uncertainty for every player, expressed as a 95% rank confidence set and the probability of being in the top *k*, together with the expected number of wrong selections at realistic cut-offs.
4. A three-tier decision rule (select, retest, do not select) and an open-source R pipeline that runs from the raw force-plate export to every number in this paper.

We analyze {{n_attempts_all}} jump attempts from {{n_athletes_all}} male soccer players tested in Mumbai, India. Section 2 describes the data and methods, Section 3 reports the results in the order of the contributions, Section 4 applies the method to a selection decision, and Sections 5 and 6 discuss limitations and conclude.

## Data

The data are routine force-plate assessments (ForceDecks, VALD Performance) of male soccer players at a single testing center in Mumbai. The battery comprised a countermovement jump (CMJ), a drop jump (DJ) and a single-leg jump (SLJ, both legs). We defined a *visit* as a day on which a player completed the whole battery. In total, {{n_athletes_all}} players had at least one visit ({{n_visits_all}} visits; {{n_attempts_all}} attempts; {{n_trial_values}} trial-level metric values; ages {{age_min_all}} to {{age_max_all}} years), and {{n_multi_visit}} players had two or more visits. We used only size-independent metrics, such as times, heights, ratios and values per kilogram of body mass, so that body size does not drive the ranking [@jaric2002]. In the released data, players carry anonymous codes, calendar dates are replaced by days since each player's first visit, and age is rounded to 0.1 year. ## Quality control and KPI selection

We judged each attempt only against the same player's other attempts in the same test, leg and visit, so that an exceptional performance is never removed for being unusual relative to other players. An attempt was removed if any of its three jump-height calculations was physically impossible (above 80 cm or at most 0 cm), or if its jump height was less than half or more than twice the player's median for that test, leg and visit. The same consistency rule was then applied to each metric value, removing only the affected value, and ranking used complete attempts only.

Candidate metrics then passed four filters. They had to be (a) relevant, which excluded protocol settings, body-weight duplicates, landing metrics and strategy measures with no agreed better direction; (b) available for at least 95% of players at their first visit, with at most 5% of values removed by quality control; (c) reliable, with an intraclass correlation (ICC) of the player's trial mean of at least 0.75 [@weir2005; @koo2016]; and (d) not redundant: working from the most to the least reliable metric, a metric was kept only if its absolute correlation with every metric already kept was below 0.70 [@dormann2013]. The ICC was computed from first-visit trials as one minus the ratio of the within-player variance of the trial mean to the between-player variance [@hopkins2000].

## Age-relative scores

Each key performance indicator (KPI) was scored in two ways. The *current* score standardizes a value against the first-visit mean and standard deviation of the whole cohort, which is what a raw leaderboard does. The *age-relative* score compares the value with players of the same age using the LMS method [@cole1992], fitted in GAMLSS as a Box–Cox Cole–Green distribution with penalized splines in age [@rigby2005]:

EQ|lms|z_{ij} = \frac{\left(y_{ij}/M_j(a_i)\right)^{L_j} - 1}{L_j\,S_j(a_i)}

Here *y*~ij~ is the value of KPI *j* for player *i* aged *a*~i~, and *M*, *S* and *L* are the age-specific median, coefficient of variation and skewness. Scores were sign-flipped where lower is better (for example, concentric duration) and were not capped.

## Current ability from repeated visits

To combine a player's visits into one estimate of current ability, we fitted a linear mixed model to each KPI [@bates2015]:

EQ|lmm|z_{ivr} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t_{iv} + b_{0i} + b_{1i} t_{iv} + u_{iv} + \varepsilon_{ivr}

where *z*~ivr~ is the score of trial *r* at visit *v* of player *i*, *t*~iv~ is the time in years before the player's latest visit, *b*~0i~ and *b*~1i~ are the player's random intercept and slope, *u*~iv~ is a visit effect and ε~ivr~ is trial-to-trial error. Because time is centered at the latest visit, β~0~ + *b*~0i~ estimates the player's level at the latest visit. In plain terms, a player tested once with inconsistent jumps is pulled toward the cohort average, while a player tested repeatedly and consistently keeps an estimate close to their own data.

## Ranking methods and validation without ground truth

The matrix of estimated KPI levels was aggregated with five methods, a weighted mean, TOPSIS [@hwang1981], SPOTIS [@dezert2020], MARCOS [@stevic2020] and PROMETHEE II [@brans1985], each with three weighting schemes: equal, entropy [@zhu2020] and MEREC [@keshavarz2021]. This gives 15 rankers. Because a player's true ability is never observed, we validated the rankers in two complementary ways. In Test A, a simulation study [@morris2019] generated {{n_sim}} cohorts with the real visit pattern, the fitted variance components and the observed correlation between KPIs, under three definitions of true ability (mean KPI level, weakest KPI, and random positive weights). We measured agreement with the true ranking by Spearman's correlation and by normalized discounted cumulative gain over the top 10 (NDCG@10), which rewards getting the best players right [@jarvelin2002]. In Test B, we held out the latest visit of each player with at least two visits and compared the ranking predicted from earlier visits with the ranking observed at the held-out visit ({{n_heldout}} players with complete data). Rankers were ranked within each check and the two tests weighted equally; the best ranker was used for all later steps.

## Rank uncertainty and selection error

We estimated uncertainty with a nonparametric bootstrap [@efron1993]. In each of *B* = {{n_boot}} replicates, whole attempts were resampled with replacement within each player, visit, test and leg; the mixed models were refitted and the players re-ranked. The probability that player *i* is truly in the top *k* is

EQ|ptop|\hat{P}_i(k) = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\left[\,R_i^{(b)} \le k\,\right]

where *R*~i~^(b)^ is the player's rank in replicate *b*. Players with P̂ ≥ 0.90 were classed as *select*, those with P̂ < 0.10 as *do not select*, and the rest as *borderline* (retest). We also computed formal 95% confidence sets for each rank with the method of Mogstad et al. [@mogstad2024]. Finally, the expected number of wrong selections among the *k* players ranked in the top *k* is

EQ|wrong|E[W_k] = \sum_{i:\,\hat{R}_i \le k} \left(1 - \hat{P}_i(k)\right)

which is a lower bound because the bootstrap captures trial-to-trial variation only. Cut-offs of 10%, 20% and 30% of the {{n_ranked}} ranked players give *k* = 19, 37 and 55; we use 20% as the main case.

# Results

Quality control removed {{qc_removed}} attempts ({{qc_impossible}} physically impossible and {{qc_inconsistent}} inconsistent with the player's own attempts) and {{values_removed}} individual metric values. Of {{n_candidates}} candidate metrics, {{n_excl_rule}} were not performance KPIs, {{n_excl_coverage}} failed coverage, {{n_excl_unstable}} were unstable and {{n_excl_redundant}} were redundant; none failed the reliability threshold. The {{n_kpis}} KPIs retained (Appendix Table A1) had ICCs of {{icc_min}} to {{icc_max}}. After {{incomplete_excluded}} incomplete attempts were excluded, {{n_ranked}} players (median age {{age_median}} years; {{n_under18}} or {{pct_under18}}% under 18) had an estimate for every KPI and were ranked.

## The ranking method matters little (Contribution 1)

Fourteen of the 15 rankers recovered simulated true ability with Spearman correlations of {{sim_rho_others_min}} to {{sim_rho_others_max}} (Figure 2). Entropy-weighted TOPSIS was the clear outlier at {{sim_rho_topsis_entropy}}. On the held-out visit, correlations ranged from {{heldout_rho_min}} to {{heldout_rho_max}}. Averaged over both tests, the best ranker was the {{winner_label}} (simulated Spearman {{sim_rho_winner}}; NDCG@10 {{ndcg_winner}}), but its lead was small: on the point estimates, every other ranker placed at least {{method_overlap_min}} of the same 37 players in the top 20% (median {{method_overlap_median}}), and all rankings correlated with it at ρ ≥ {{method_rho_min}}. SPOTIS with equal weights produced the same ranking as the equal-weight average. Entropy weights were not aligned with reliability (rank correlation with ICC {{entropy_icc_spearman}}); the second-largest entropy weight went to the least reliable KPI, CMJ eccentric peak force (ICC 0.77; weight 0.126; Appendix Table A1).

## Age adjustment changes who is selected (Contribution 2)

Age adjustment mattered far more than the method (Figure 3). Of the {{k_main}} players in the top 20% on current scores, only {{topk_overlap}} remained there after age adjustment, and the median age of the top 20% fell from {{median_age_topk_current}} to {{median_age_topk_age}} years. The {{dropped_n}} players who dropped out had a median age of {{dropped_median_age}} years. Conversely, {{hidden_talent}} players outside the current top 20% had at least an even chance of being in the age-adjusted top 20%; their median age was {{hidden_median_age}} years and {{hidden_under18}} were under 18. The rank correlation between the current and age-adjusted rankings was only {{rank_cor_current_age}}.

## Ranks are uncertain where decisions are made (Contribution 3)

The ranking is clear at the extremes but uncertain near the cut-off (Figure 4). The median width of the 95% rank confidence set was {{range_width_median}} places across all players and {{range_width_top}} places within the top 20%. At the 20% cut-off, {{select_20}} players had P̂ ≥ 0.90, {{reject_20}} had P̂ < 0.10, and {{border_20}} ({{cohort_share_border}}% of the cohort) were borderline; only {{clearly_top_20}} players had a formal 95% rank set lying entirely inside the top 20%. Even with the best method, the expected number of wrong selections was at least {{wrong_20}} of 37. The pattern was the same at 10% and 30% (Table 1).

## Sensitivity

The conclusions were robust to the main analysis choices. Without age adjustment, the 20% cut-off gave {{cur_select_20}} select, {{cur_border_20}} borderline and at least {{cur_wrong_20}} expected wrong selections, so uncertainty near the cut-off is not an artifact of age scoring. Combining visits with the mixed model did not predict the held-out visit better than the latest single visit (age-relative Spearman {{long_b_age}} versus {{latest_b_age}}; current {{long_b_cur}} versus {{latest_b_cur}}), but it was slightly better in simulation ({{long_a}} versus {{latest_a}}), so its main value is to use every visit without discarding data. Finally, we reran the full pipeline on two computing platforms and two versions of the mixed-model software (four runs, listed in the repository). The top-ranked method alternated among near-tied equal-weight and entropy-weight methods (Figure 2), but the 20% decisions were identical in every run (29 select, 16 borderline, 138 do not select), with 2.19 to 2.22 expected wrong selections.

# Application: Reading a Leaderboard with Uncertainty

Table 2 shows how the method changes a selection meeting. It lists the players around the 20% cut-off with their current rank, their age-adjusted rank, its 95% rank set and the probability of being in the top 37. A raw leaderboard would draw a line under rank 37. Our leaderboard instead gives three groups. Players marked *select* are in the top 37 in at least 90% of bootstrap replicates and can be offered places now. Players marked *do not select* are almost never in the top 37 and can be offered a development pathway instead. The borderline group, which reaches from rank {{border_rank_min}} to rank {{border_rank_max}}, should be retested before any decision. Rank alone hides this: the players ranked 38th and 39th have probabilities of 0.31 and 0.39 of truly belonging in the top 37, while the player ranked 26th has only 0.80 and is not yet a safe selection. Retesting concentrates staff time on {{border_20}} players rather than the whole pool.

# Discussion

In these data, how the composite is built was the least important choice: once KPIs were chosen for reliability and non-redundancy, the simplest equal-weight average was among the best of 15 rankers. The one poor performer, entropy-weighted TOPSIS, pairs weights that reward dispersion, which in repeated testing partly reflects measurement noise [@hopkins2000], with a distance-based method sensitive to extreme values. Age adjustment mattered far more; without it, the leaderboard mainly selected the oldest players, echoing the relative-age and maturation biases documented in youth soccer [@cobley2009; @helsen2005; @hill2020]. Most importantly, uncertainty is largest exactly where decisions are made. As with institutional league tables [@goldstein1996; @mogstad2024], players far from the cut-off can be classified with confidence but a sizable middle group cannot, and even a well-designed ranking is expected to make at least {{wrong_20}} wrong selections out of 37.

We therefore recommend three practices for talent programs. First, choose KPIs for reliability and non-redundancy and combine them with equal weights. Second, rank players against same-age peers whenever the pool spans several ages. Third, report every rank with its uncertainty and use select, retest and do-not-select groups instead of a single line.

**Limitations.** The data come from male players at one testing center, and the age models are fitted to this cohort rather than to external norms. Jump tests capture only part of soccer performance, and no measure of later success was available, so the analysis shows how certain a ranking is, not whether it predicts careers. Retesting in this center was unrelated to first-visit scores (AUC {{ret_auc}} among {{ret_n}} players with a year of possible follow-up), so later attendance cannot validate the ranking. Maturity status was not measured, so age-relative scores do not remove maturation differences within an age [@malina2015; @cumming2017]. The bootstrap ignores day-to-day variation, so the true selection error is larger than our lower bound, and the 0.90 and 0.10 thresholds are conventions that programs may change. Future work should add maturity offset, technical and tactical assessments, female players and several centers, and link rankings to later selection and match performance.

# Conclusion

We have shown how to turn a physical-test leaderboard into a selection rule that respects measurement error and age. In {{n_ranked}} male soccer players, the choice among 15 ranking methods mattered little, age adjustment replaced most of the top 20%, and at a 20% cut-off only {{select_20}} players could be selected with confidence, {{border_20}} needed retesting and at least {{wrong_20}} wrong selections were expected from a single ranking.

Coaches, scouts and sport scientists who rank players on physical tests can use the framework directly: combine reliable KPIs with equal weights, compare players with same-age peers, and retest the borderline group before committing long-term support. The code, the de-identified data and the variable dictionary needed to apply it to another test battery are available at https://github.com/praveenmaths89/athlete-ranking-uncertainty.
